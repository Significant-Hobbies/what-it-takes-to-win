## Introduction: The limits of the single score

When we attempt to explain why a particular individual achieved a notable early-career breakthrough, the conversation often devolves into binary extremes. On one side is the myth of pure, unassisted merit, which ignores the reality of differing starting lines. On the other side is the over-correction: the attempt to quantify every aspect of an individual’s background into a single, comprehensive score.

While attempting to acknowledge disparity is useful, compressing a life’s complex starting conditions into a single number deletes the mechanics of how advantage actually works. When you look closely at the trajectories of individuals, you see that early conditions do not operate as a monolithic force. They are distinct vectors that interact with perseverance and timing in observable ways.

Our dataset at Look Sideways consists of 3,578 documented early-breakthrough paths. To explain these outcomes without resorting to deterministic formulas, we require a framework that preserves the nuance of different starting conditions. This led to the development of a three-source framework for explaining advantage.

## The three-source framework

Every public explanation of advantage in our research uses one three-source structure. By breaking down early conditions into distinct categories, we can observe how starting position changes where effort lands without judging the intensity of that effort or the merit of the individual.

The three condition factors are:
1. **What they brought**
2. **What they were handed**
3. **What surrounded them**

These are not predictive variables in a causal formula; they are an explanatory mechanism to understand the environment in which perseverance and luck operate.

## Source 1: What they brought

The first source of advantage consists of the intrinsic traits, early predispositions, and natural inclinations an individual possesses. These are the internal conditions that a person brings to their environment.

In the context of the 3,578 paths we track, "what they brought" often manifests as early obsession, atypical physical attributes for athletes, or an unusual tolerance for specific types of cognitive strain. It is crucial to document this because intrinsic traits do not guarantee an outcome; they simply provide leverage when matched with the right environment.

For example, a high tolerance for risk is an advantage for a founder, but it remains a latent trait unless the individual is also handed a safety net or surrounded by an ecosystem that rewards risk-taking. By isolating what a person brought, we can see how internal traits require external activation.

## Source 2: What they were handed

The second source encompasses the immediate, unearned external conditions an individual receives at the start of their path. This includes family resources, financial stability, early access to specific education, and baseline safety nets.

When people talk about advantage, they are most often referring to this source. However, what people are handed is not always an advantage. In our dataset, we explicitly track conditions on a scale from −1 to 3. The inclusion of the −1 score is vital because it documents active headwinds. Across our records, we observed 458 specific headwind readings—situations where an individual was handed structural disadvantages, requiring them to expend significant effort just to reach a neutral starting line.

If we only tracked positive advantages, we would collapse these 458 headwinds into a zero, falsely equating a neutral starting position with an actively detrimental one. Documenting exactly what someone was handed, including the deficits, is the only way to accurately map the distance their subsequent effort had to travel.

## Source 3: What surrounded them

The third source of advantage is often the most invisible: the broader context, geography, timing, and network proximity that envelop an individual. Unlike what someone is handed, what surrounds them is environmental and systemic.

Being in the right city during a technological boom, coming of age during a specific economic era, or having indirect proximity to industry hubs are massive drivers of early breakthroughs. A founder in 1995 faced a different set of environmental multipliers than a founder in 2015.

In our dataset of 1,390 founders and operators, environmental timing frequently acts as the dividing line between those who achieved T1 (653 records) or T2 (1,254 records) outcomes and those who faced prolonged friction. What surrounds an individual dictates the conversion rate of their effort. You can bring exceptional traits and be handed decent resources, but if you are surrounded by an environment with a low ceiling for your specific pursuit, your upward trajectory will inevitably cap out sooner.

## Why we never sum the factors

The most important rule of the three-source framework is that these factors are never summed into a single score.

When you sum "what they brought," "what they were handed," and "what surrounded them," you create a meaningless aggregate. A score of 4 could mean a person brought extraordinary intrinsic traits but faced severe headwinds (3 + -1 + 2), or it could mean they had moderate traits, were handed significant wealth, and lived in an isolated environment (1 + 3 + 0). Combining these numbers destroys the specific narrative of the path.

Our equal-effort/different-distance exhibit exists entirely to demonstrate this. If two people put in the exact same amount of perseverance, the distance they travel is dictated by the specific combination of the three sources. By keeping the condition factors separate, we maintain a direct, humane, and evidence-bounded view of reality. The factors remain qualitative indicators of the environment, not a definitive percentile or a verdict on a person's worth.

## The intersection of conditions, perseverance, and luck

Advantage alone does not produce outcomes. The three sources merely set the stage for the two dynamic variables that dictate actual trajectories: perseverance and luck.

Perseverance is the documented effort, the repeated attempts, and the navigation of sequence. It crosses through the three sources, converting potential advantage into kinetic leverage. However, perseverance is notoriously difficult to measure objectively. We do not attempt to score perseverance; instead, we document it as path evidence.

Luck is similarly unscored but explicitly categorized. Through our examination of sourced, ordinary-person cases, we identify four forms of luck that intersect with the three condition factors:
- **Structural luck:** Being born in a specific era or geographic location.
- **Encounter luck:** The chance meeting that alters a trajectory.
- **Event luck:** A forced door, a visa draw, or an unpredictable exogenous shock.
- **Variance luck:** The statistical noise of repeated attempts.

By keeping luck unscored and conditions separated, the framework prevents the outcome from becoming a repeatable identity. It shows that an early breakthrough is the result of specific interactions, not a causal formula that can be guaranteed by accumulating enough advantages.

## Escaping the comparison trap

The ultimate purpose of the three-source framework is not to build a better database, but to release individuals from the trap of unhelpful comparison.

When readers arrive at our exhibits after turning another person's visible outcome into a judgment about their own pace or ability, they need a credible explanation of why that comparison is structurally flawed. The three-source framework provides that explanation. It shows, with specific numbers and inspectable evidence, that the unobserved paths that did not survive faced different headwinds, lacked specific environmental multipliers, or encountered different variance.

Comparison is futile as a verdict. Understanding the three sources of advantage allows us to look sideways at others' paths, not as benchmarks for our own worth, but as distinct trajectories shaped by conditions we do not share.

## Next Action

Apply this framework to a recent comparison you've made. Rather than judging your progress against someone else's visible outcome, separate their starting conditions into what they brought, what they were handed, and what surrounded them. Then, identify the specific forms of encounter or event luck that accelerated their path, and recognize how their unique combination of sources makes direct comparison functionally impossible.
