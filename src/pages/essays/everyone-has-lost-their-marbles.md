Let me start with myself.

I have spent too much time looking at someone else's position and turning it into a judgment about mine.

They built more by my age, so I must have wasted mine.
They are moving faster, so I must be less capable.
Their marble is ahead, so mine must be defective.

But that conclusion rests on an absurd premise:

That we were released from the same place, onto the same track, as the same marble.

You have probably seen those reels where coloured marbles race through elaborate obstacle courses. They fall through funnels, bounce off pegs, squeeze through gates, collide with one another, and accelerate down different paths.

Now make the race more like life.

Drop some marbles onto steep, clear lanes that generate momentum immediately. Place others inside crowded funnels where moving at all requires effort.

Give some hidden boosts. Polish others until their surfaces become smoother and they lose less energy at every collision. Remove obstacles before certain marbles arrive. Make others spend half the race escaping pits that the leaders never encountered.

That is closer to the world we enter.

Some people begin with money, health, confidence, education, supportive parents, useful relationships, and early exposure to the right ideas.

Some acquire smoother surfaces over time. Skills, savings, reputation, experience, and communication reduce friction. They do not eliminate future obstacles, but they make each one easier to cross.

Some never face problems that dominate other people's lives. They do not need to worry about rent, recover from a poor education, care for an ill parent, or sacrifice the promising option for the safe one.

This does not mean the leading marble never worked hard.

It means its work acted upon a different trajectory.

Other marbles matter too.

A destructive relationship, dishonest partner, cynical friend, or bad manager can drain years of momentum. A teacher, friend, collaborator, or stranger can arrive at exactly the right angle and push someone onto a path they could not have reached alone.

We are not merely moving through the course. We are constantly altering one another's trajectories.

Then there is luck.

One marble reaches a gate just before it closes. Another lands on the wrong side of a peg. A tiny early deflection changes its momentum, which changes the next obstacle it encounters, which changes everything that follows.

By the time we compare two people, we are not comparing two isolated individuals.

We are comparing two accumulated histories.

Different beginnings. Different boosts. Different collisions. Different responsibilities. Different timing. Different costs. Different information. Different luck.

Comparison as judgment is not merely unhealthy.

It is logically broken.

It removes nearly every cause that produced the result, keeps only the visible score, and then pretends the score explains something.

It does not.

Two people can make equally good decisions and reach different outcomes. One person can make poor decisions and still win because the course favoured them. Another can make excellent decisions and lose because several unlikely obstacles arrived together.

Even two people of the same age, from the same college, working in the same field are not running the same race.

Age is a timestamp, not a shared starting line.

Another person's outcome cannot tell you what your outcome should have been. It cannot prove that you worked less, wanted less, chose worse, or possessed less ability.

Sometimes those things may be true.

But their position cannot establish it.

Their success can reveal that a path exists. Their methods can teach you. Their mistakes can warn you. Their choices can expose possibilities you had not considered.

But their result cannot tell you whether the same destination was available to you, at the same time, for the same cost, with the same odds.

Their life is evidence about the world.

It is not a verdict on yours.

The marble metaphor, however, can also become too comforting.

Humans are not ordinary marbles. We can observe the course, learn from previous collisions, develop skills, choose better companions, change environments, and sometimes move onto another track.

But we are not magical marbles either.

We cannot roll anywhere merely because we believe hard enough.

Agency is real, but bounded.

Health, talent, geography, time, obligations, previous decisions, money, and chance place genuine limits around what remains possible. Some paths close. Some were never available. Most people will not become extreme outliers, regardless of how sincerely or intensely they try.

Effort changes probabilities.

It does not issue guarantees.

Telling everyone they can achieve anything is not kindness. It turns probability into morality. When the promised outcome fails to appear, people do not blame the promise. They blame their discipline, intelligence, courage, or worth.

Hope without credible possibility becomes a machine for self-blame.

Hope does not require certainty. But it requires a plausible path.

Sometimes maturity is not believing harder. It is grieving a path that has genuinely closed and choosing one that remains alive.

None of this means standards are futile.

You can still be behind.

Not behind another person—but behind what your own available choices could reasonably have produced.

You may be wasting time. Avoiding discomfort. Remaining in a destructive environment. Refusing to develop a skill you know you need. Protecting an impossible fantasy because accepting reality would hurt.

But you cannot diagnose any of that by staring at someone else's scoreboard.

You have to inspect your own pattern.

The honest question is not:

> Why am I not where they are?

It is:

> Given where I actually began, what I now know, what I can influence, and what remains plausibly open, am I improving my trajectory—or abandoning it?

That is the standard I can live by.

This is a love letter to anyone who has turned another person's momentum into evidence against themselves, starting with me.

You do not need to become statistically exceptional for your life to count.

A better trajectory may mean greater autonomy, steadier health, meaningful work, deeper relationships, financial security, or simply fewer days spent living against yourself.

These are not consolation prizes.

They are most of life.

And this is not permission to drift. Your choices matter precisely because your possibilities are finite. A bounded life makes attention more important, not less.

Look at other people for information. Study what worked, what failed, what it cost, and which doors may still exist.

The scoreboard is useless.

The footage can still teach you.

Let another person's success expand your map, not become an accusation against you.

Everyone has their own trajectory.

Not infinite.
Not fixed.
Not comparable.

Look sideways for information, never for a verdict.

Then return to your own course and make the best next move that is still genuinely available.
